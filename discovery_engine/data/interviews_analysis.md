## Q1. What kinds of old photos do users struggle to retrieve?

Users struggle most with retrieving photos of milestone moments such as a child’s firsts (first steps, first haircut, first birthday cake smash) and culturally specific occasions (first Holi, Diwali kite play). The difficulty stems from sparse or ambiguous metadata—keywords like "first" or "Holi" are too generic, and the app’s face‑recognition or location filters often mislabel or miss the target image, forcing users to scroll endlessly.

- [P1] "I typed “Anaya park 2019” because the only concrete bits I could pull together were her name, the park we went to and the year I was pretty sure of."
- [P1] "When the “Anaya park 2019” search just gave me a bunch of generic swing‑and‑slide shots, I thought the AI wasn’t picking up the kite because it wasn’t in the metadata."
- [P1] "I tapped the Location filter, typed “Cubbon Park”, and hit Apply. That narrowed the set to about 12 pictures, and finally one of them showed the red kite stuck in the low tree with Anaya’s hand on the string."
- [P2] "The app still shows the label “unknown”, which is annoying, but the face is clearly his, not my nephew’s."
- [P3] "Because I knew it was from the Diwali period, I opened the filter pane and set the month to Nov 2019."
- [P3] "Honestly, it’s a bit maddening – I feel that little knot in my stomach when I’m racing the clock for the slideshow and the search just throws me a sea of unrelated pictures."

**Public feedback:** mixed – some public posts echo the difficulty with generic keywords, while others note that the app sometimes mislabels or omits key images

*Confidence: medium*

## Q2. What information do people actually remember about a photo?

People most reliably recall the visual details of a photo—such as a bright red kite or a frosting‑covered face—alongside who was present and where the moment took place.  Time‑specific cues (exact dates or relative events like “Diwali 2019”) are remembered less often, and only a few participants recall the broader occasion or emotional tone.  Overall, the interview data show that visual content and people are the strongest memory anchors, while time and emotion are weaker.

- [P1] "I just remembered it was that park day in early 2019 – we were at Cubbon Park and Anaya grabbed a red kite"
- [P2] "the date stamp says 15 Feb 2022"
- [P3] "the red kite was the standout object in the memory"
- [P4] "I remembered the red dust, the yellow shirt, and that he was holding a stick of cotton candy like a prize."
- [P5] "they were in their bright blue shirts, holding a stuffed giraffe, standing in the school hallway"
- [P6] "she was three on August 15, 2019"

**Public feedback:** mixed – public forums and app reviews show lower recall of visual content and people compared to interviews, but agree on the relative importance of time cues.

*Confidence: medium*

## Q3. What information have they forgotten?

The participants most often forgot the exact place of the event (4 of 9), followed by the exact date (2 of 9). They also missed the month (1 of 9) and the visual angle of the kite (1 of 9).

- [P2] "I think it was ours, but I’m not 100 % on that"
- [P4] "The place was my parents’ house in Gurgaon, but I never noted the address or the exact location on the map."
- [P1] "I had no clue which day it was – I just knew it was sometime in the first half of the year"
- [P3] "I only knew it was sometime in November, not the specific date"
- [P3] "I wasn’t sure whether she was holding the kite, the kite was draped over the sofa, or she was looking up at it"

**Public feedback:** mixed – some dimensions (place, time_absolute, visual_content) show higher recall in public reviews, while time_relative is consistent across sources

*Confidence: medium*

## Q4. How do users formulate searches when their memory is incomplete?

When memory is fragmentary, users first translate remembered pieces into keyword searches, then refine with dates, locations, or people. For example, P1 combined a name, place and year into "Anaya park 2019", P3 started with the object "kite" and added colour and date to get "red kite" and "Nov 2019", and P5 turned a vague scene into "playschool 2023" and later "giraffe play school 2022". These queries show a step‑wise build from fragments to precise terms.

- [P1] "Anaya park 2019"
- [P1, P3] "kite"
- [P1, P3] "red kite"
- [P5] "playschool 2023"
- [P5] "giraffe play school 2022"

**Public feedback:** mixed – interviewers use keyword strategies more often than public posts, but natural‑language queries are more common in public feedback.

*Confidence: medium*

## Q5. Where does the search break, and what do people do when it fails?

The search fails most often when the system’s evaluation of the query does not match the user’s mental model—three participants (P1, P5, P6) reported that the app returned many irrelevant or blurry results, forcing them to scroll or abandon the search. One participant (P4) experienced an understanding mismatch, where the app’s generic results did not align with the memory of a specific event. When the search fails, users either refine the query with additional filters (P1, P2), manually scroll through results (P5, P6), or give up and send a substitute photo (P5, P6).

- [P5] "I’ll keep looking tomorrow. I don’t know, it’s the only one I found. It felt like a mini‑panic, honestly."
- [P6] "It was kinda frustrating, honestly. I was stuck scrolling and scrolling"

**Public feedback:** differs – public posts report a much higher share of UNDERSTANDING failures (≈60%) than our sample (≈11%).

*Confidence: medium*

## Surprises and contradictions

- Users struggle most with retrieving milestone photos despite having strong visual memory anchors
- The app’s face‑recognition and location filters often mislabel or omit target images, contradicting users’ expectation of accurate metadata
- Public feedback shows higher rates of understanding failures than the interview sample, indicating a discrepancy between real‑world usage and controlled studies

## Open questions for real interviews

- What specific metadata (e.g., color, angle, event type) most effectively disambiguates ambiguous keywords like "first" or "kite"?
- How can the search system better align its evaluation of queries with users’ mental models to reduce irrelevant or blurry results?
